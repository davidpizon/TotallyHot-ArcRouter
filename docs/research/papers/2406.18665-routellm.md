## Abstract

> Large language models (LLMs) excel at a wide range of tasks, but choosing the right model often involves balancing performance and cost. Powerful models offer better results but are expensive, while smaller models are more cost-effective but less capable. To address this trade-off, we introduce a training framework for learning efficient router models that dynamically select between a stronger and weaker LLM during inference. Our framework leverages human preference data and employs data augmentation techniques to enhance performance. Evaluations on public benchmarks show that our approach can reduce costs by over 2 times without sacrificing response quality. Moreover, our routers exhibit strong generalization capabilities, maintaining performance even when routing between LLMs not included in training. This highlights the potential of our framework to deliver cost-effective, high-performance LLM solutions.

## Method

The paper studies binary routing between a strong model class and a weak model class. Preference labels are win-strong, tie, or win-weak. A win-prediction model estimates the probability that the strong model wins on a query. A threshold α in [0, 1] turns that probability into a decision: a higher α sends more queries to the weak model.

Training preferences come from about 80k Chatbot Arena battles. Labels between any two models are sparse (under 0.1% on average), so models are clustered into 10 tiers from the Arena leaderboard. The top two tiers stand in for the strong class and the third tier for the weak class. Responses are dropped; only winner identities are kept. Prompts shorter than 16 characters are pruned, leaving about 65k pairwise comparisons across 64 models, with 5k held out for validation.

Two augmentations fill in the sparse labels. Golden-label augmentation uses the MMLU validation split (about 1,500 questions) and marks a win by comparing each model's answer with the known answer. Judge augmentation builds about 120k labels by taking Nectar queries that already have GPT-4 responses, generating Mixtral-8x7B responses, and scoring the pair with a GPT-4 judge. The paper reports that collection cost about $700.

Section 4.2 defines four win predictors:

- **Similarity-weighted ranking.** A Bradley-Terry model solved at inference time. Query similarity is a scaled cosine on text embeddings. Each training query is weighted by γ raised to one plus that similarity, with γ = 10. No separate training run.
- **Matrix factorization.** A bilinear score from a learned model vector and a projected query embedding, passed through a sigmoid. Trained about 10 epochs on an 8GB GPU, batch size 64, Adam at learning rate 3×10⁻⁴ and weight decay 1×10⁻⁵.
- **BERT classifier.** BERT-base, with the win probability a sigmoid over the CLS embedding. Full fine-tuning, about 2,000 steps, batch size 16, sequence length 512, learning rate 1×10⁻⁵, weight decay 0.01, on 2×L4 24GB GPUs.
- **Causal LLM.** Llama 3 8B predicts the win probability as a softmax over label tokens appended to the vocabulary. About 2,000 steps, batch size 8, sequence length 2,048, learning rate 1×10⁻⁶, on 8×A100 80GB GPUs.

Matrix factorization and similarity-weighted ranking embed queries with OpenAI `text-embedding-3-small`. The reported pair is `gpt-4-1106-preview` against Mixtral 8x7B. The baseline is a random router under the same cost constraint.

Metrics, from §3.2: the percentage of calls sent to the strong model; performance gap recovered (PGR), the fraction of the weak-to-strong quality gap the router gets back; average PGR (APGR) across cost levels; and call-performance threshold CPT(x%), the minimum strong-model call rate needed to recover x% of the gap.

## Key results

Numbers below are those stated in the HTML text that was read. Several tables are cited by the paper without their cell values being repeated in the surrounding prose, so those cells are not copied here.

- On MT Bench, matrix factorization and similarity-weighted ranking trained on Arena data beat the random router. Matrix factorization needs half as many GPT-4 calls as random to reach PGR 50%. BERT and the causal LLM stay near random on Arena data alone. The paper attributes that to capacity in a sparse-data regime. Adding the judge augmentation improves all four. Matrix factorization then has its CPT(80%) nearly halved and uses 50% fewer GPT-4 calls than random. The MT Bench score at CPT(50%) is given as 8.8, which the paper says is 95% of GPT-4's 9.3.
- On 5-shot MMLU, Arena-only training is near random. Adding the roughly 1,500 golden MMLU validation items (under 2% of the training data) makes every router need about 20% fewer GPT-4 calls than random at CPT(50%). The score at CPT(50%) is given as 75, 92% of GPT-4's 81.
- On 8-shot GSM8K, Arena-only training is again near random. Judge augmentation moves every router from an APGR worse than random to an APGR better than random. The causal LLM needs 17% fewer GPT-4 calls than random at CPT(50%) and CPT(80%). The score at CPT(50%) is given as 75, 87% of GPT-4's 86.
- The same routers, without retraining, are applied to Claude 3 Opus versus Claude 3 Sonnet, and to Llama 3.1 70B versus Llama 3.1 8B. The paper reports performance comparable to the original pair, and the best routers needing about half the strong-model calls of the random router at CPT(80%).
- Appendix D prices GPT-4 at $24.7 per million tokens and Mixtral 8x7B at $0.24 per million tokens. Table 6's cost-saving ratio, defined as the inverse of the best router's GPT-4 call rate relative to random, reaches 3.66×. The abstract's "over 2 times" is the weaker of these statements.
- Routing overhead (Table 7, described in §5.5): GPU routers run on a g2-standard-4 with one L4; similarity-weighted ranking runs on an n2-standard-8. The most expensive router, similarity-weighted ranking, adds no more than 0.4% of the cost of GPT-4 generation.

The cost control is the threshold α, not a price feature inside the win model. Dollar figures are a static per-million-token estimate used after the fact.
