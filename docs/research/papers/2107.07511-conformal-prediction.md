## Abstract

> Black-box machine learning models are now routinely used in high-risk settings, like medical diagnostics, which demand uncertainty quantification to avoid consequential model failures. Conformal prediction (a.k.a. conformal inference) is a user-friendly paradigm for creating statistically rigorous uncertainty sets/intervals for the predictions of such models. Critically, the sets are valid in a distribution-free sense: they possess explicit, non-asymptotic guarantees even without distributional assumptions or model assumptions. One can use conformal prediction with any pre-trained model, such as a neural network, to produce sets that are guaranteed to contain the ground truth with a user-specified probability, such as 90%. It is easy-to-understand, easy-to-use, and general, applying naturally to problems arising in the fields of computer vision, natural language processing, deep reinforcement learning, and so on. This hands-on introduction is aimed to provide the reader a working understanding of conformal prediction and related distribution-free uncertainty quantification techniques with one self-contained document. We lead the reader through practical theory for and examples of conformal prediction and describe its extensions to complex machine learning tasks involving structured outputs, distribution shift, time-series, outliers, models that abstain, and more. Throughout, there are many explanatory illustrations, examples, and code samples in Python. ## Method

Split conformal prediction, which the paper treats as the usual practical version, starts from any already-fitted model and a held-out calibration set. It does not retrain the model.

The classification walk-through in §1:

1. Fit a classifier that emits a softmax vector. The softmax is only a heuristic uncertainty.
2. Hold out n fresh i.i.d. labeled points the model did not train on. The illustration uses n around 500.
3. Score each calibration point as one minus the softmax probability of the true class. A high score means the model was wrong or unsure about the truth.
4. Let q-hat be the ceiling of (n+1)(1−α), divided by n, empirical quantile of those scores.
5. For a new input, the prediction set is every class whose softmax probability is at least 1 − q-hat.

The general procedure in §1.1 replaces the softmax score with any score s(x, y) that is large when x and y disagree. The prediction set is every y whose score is at most q-hat. If the calibration points and the test point are i.i.d., the set contains the true label with probability at least 1 − α, for any score and any data distribution. The paper calls this marginal coverage: the probability is an average over calibration and test draws, not a guarantee for one particular input. A small finite-sample correction (the +1 inside the ceiling) is what makes the inequality hold. An upper bound needs a tie-breaking step that the paper says is usually skipped in practice. The proof is in Appendix D.

Later sections extend the same idea. Headings, which were read as an outline rather than line by line, cover adaptive classification sets, conformalized quantile regression, conformalizing a scalar uncertainty estimate, conformalizing Bayes, group-balanced and class-conditional sets, conformal risk control, outlier detection, covariate shift, distribution drift, full conformal prediction, cross-conformal prediction, CV+, and jackknife+. Section 5.5 is selective classification, the paper's treatment of models that abstain. Those extensions are named here so a reader can find them. This summary does not restate their theorems.

## Key results

The result this summary relies on is the coverage statement above, not a benchmark table. The paper is a tutorial. Its numerical illustrations (ImageNet prediction sets, a calibration set of about 500 points, a 90% example) are examples of the guarantee, not a claim that any particular router achieves 90% coverage.

Two limits matter for later use. Coverage is marginal, so a set can be systematically too small on a subgroup and still average out. Coverage assumes the calibration points and the test point are exchangeable. A shift between them, including a shift the paper treats in §4.5 and §4.6, is outside the basic theorem.
