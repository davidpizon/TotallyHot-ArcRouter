## Abstract

> Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated exceptional performance across a wide range of natural language tasks. However, selecting the optimal LLM to respond to a user query often necessitates a delicate balance between performance and cost. While powerful models deliver better results, they come at a high cost, whereas smaller models are more cost-effective but less capable. To address this trade-off, we propose IRT-Router, a multi-LLM routing framework that efficiently routes user queries to the most suitable LLM. Inspired by Item Response Theory (IRT), a psychological measurement methodology, IRT-Router explicitly models the relationship between LLM capabilities and user query attributes. This not only enables accurate prediction of response performance but also provides interpretable insights, such as LLM abilities and query difficulty. Additionally, we design an online query warm-up technique based on semantic similarity, further enhancing the online generalization capability of IRT-Router. Extensive experiments on 20 LLMs and 12 datasets demonstrate that IRT-Router outperforms most baseline methods in terms of effectiveness and interpretability. Its superior performance in cold-start scenarios further confirms the reliability and practicality of IRT-Router in real-world applications. Code is available at https://github.com/Mercidaiha/IRT-Router .

## Method

Item response theory treats each LLM as a test-taker and each query as an item. A monotonic link says that a higher ability parameter raises predicted performance on a query. The paper uses that link so the router can report an ability and a difficulty, not only a winner.

Each query is embedded with `bert-base-uncased`. Each LLM is embedded from a profile (release date, developer, type, features, and a short description), with the same encoder.

Two predictors share that embedding:

- **MIRT-Router.** A multidimensional logistic model. An LLM has an ability vector, and a query has a discrimination vector and a scalar difficulty, each produced by a linear map of the embedding. Predicted performance is the logistic of their interaction. Training minimizes binary cross-entropy against an empirical score from comparing the LLM's answer with the ground truth.
- **NIRT-Router.** Adds an explicit relevance vector over predefined abilities. On the training queries, embeddings are reduced with UMAP and clustered with HDBSCAN. For each cluster, an LLM labels the abilities required by five sample questions, and the relevance vector is 0/1 over those abilities. A test query takes the mean relevance of its five nearest training neighbors. A neural interaction layer then combines ability, discrimination, difficulty, and relevance. The loss is the same binary cross-entropy.

The routing score is α times predicted performance plus β times a cost term, with α + β = 1. The cost term is not this request's token bill. Section 3.1 defines it as a fixed linear map of the LLM's output price into [0, 1]: GPT-4o's output price of $10 per million tokens maps to 1, and DeepSeek-Chat's $0.28 per million maps to 0.028. The paper says this fixed cost is "the most basic representation" and that a user could set a self-hosted model's cost to 0. The query is sent to the LLM with the highest score.

Cold start for an unseen query blends the query vector with the average embedding of its k nearest training queries. The reported k is 5. Ability dimension N is 25 for both routers. Training uses Adam at learning rate 0.002 and batch size 512, on one A100 40GB.

The interaction set pairs 20 LLMs with 12 datasets. Eight datasets (MMLU, CMMLU, ACLUE, ARC-Challenge, HotpotQA, SQuAD, MATH, MBPP) are split 70/30 before any LLM is called, and the splits are pooled into train and in-distribution test. Four further datasets (C-Eval, CommonsenseQA, GSM8K, HumanEval) are out of distribution. Baselines include always-small (Ministral-8B-Instruct-2410), always-large (GPT-4o), HybridLLM and RouteLLM as binary routers on that pair, and RouterBench's predictive router.

## Key results

From §6.1, as stated in the prose (the table cells themselves are not reproduced here):

- In the in-distribution settings, IRT-Router has the highest performance. Average answer accuracy is 3% higher than GPT-4o alone, at 1/30 of GPT-4o's total cost. Against the small model alone, performance is 32% higher at a comparable total cost.
- IRT-Router also has the best reward when performance and cost are balanced. At α = 0.8 it costs half of the RouterBench predictive router in that comparison. MIRT and NIRT are close; MIRT is slightly ahead in distribution, NIRT slightly ahead out of distribution. Out of distribution, performance is 2% higher than the best baseline.
- Ability values line up with model size in the reported pairs: Llama 3.1 70B Instruct is at least Llama 3.1 8B Instruct on each plotted dimension (average in-distribution performance 71% versus 32%). Query difficulty from MIRT matches MATH level labels on the two illustrated items.
- Under α = 0.8, the hardest 30% of in-distribution test queries go to DeepSeek-Chat 80% of the time, and the easiest 30% go to Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct-GPTQ-Int4 99% of the time. The paper gives those models' output prices as $0.28 and $0.2 per million tokens.
- Top-1 routing accuracy is described as relatively low, because several models score similarly once cost is included and the router is deliberately small. A new LLM, Claude 3.5 Haiku 20241022, is predicted on the in-distribution test with accuracy 0.67. The paper calls generalization to unseen LLMs limited. Removing the warm-up lowers reward on the out-of-distribution set, more so for NIRT.

The performance model does not take the price as an input. Price enters only as the fixed per-model cost inside the score, with α and β chosen in advance.
