## Abstract

> As the range of applications for Large Language Models (LLMs) continues to grow, the demand for effective serving solutions becomes increasingly critical. Despite the versatility of LLMs, no single model can optimally address all tasks and applications, particularly when balancing performance with cost. This limitation has led to the development of LLM routing systems, which combine the strengths of various models to overcome the constraints of individual LLMs. Yet, the absence of a standardized benchmark for evaluating the performance of LLM routers hinders progress in this area. To bridge this gap, we present RouterBench, a novel evaluation framework designed to systematically assess the efficacy of LLM routing systems, along with a comprehensive dataset comprising over 405k inference outcomes from representative LLMs to support the development of routing strategies. We further propose a theoretical framework for LLM routing, and deliver a comparative analysis of various routing approaches through RouterBench, highlighting their potentials and limitations within our evaluation framework. This work not only formalizes and advances the development of LLM routing systems but also sets a standard for their assessment, paving the way for more accessible and economically viable LLM deployments. The code and data are available at https://github.com/withmartian/routerbench .

## Method

Each model, on each example, has a generation cost and a quality. A router is a function of the prompt and of parameters θ (a willingness to pay, a latency limit, or the router's own architecture). Sweeping θ produces a cloud of (cost, quality) points.

Two constructions turn that cloud into one curve. Linear interpolation mixes two routers at random so the expected cost and quality land on the segment between them. Extrapolation reaches any cost in [0, ∞): extra cost with no extra quality extends the curve upward, and mixing with a null router (zero cost, zero quality) extends it downward. The non-decreasing convex hull keeps only the points that are not dominated by a mixture. The Zero router is the hull of the individual models: for a target cost it mixes models and never uses a learned router. A learned router has to beat that hull to matter.

Average Improvement in Quality (AIQ) is the area under a system's hull between a shared c-min and c-max, divided by the width of that cost range.

The dataset has 405,467 rows. The prose in §4.3 says these cover 11 models, 8 datasets, and 64 tasks. The model list in the same section names 14 systems, three of them only on the RAG slice (sonar-small-online, sonar-medium-online, and the You.com API), so the "11" and the "14" are both in the paper and refer to different cuts. Open models named: Llama-70B-chat, Mixtral-8x7B-chat, Yi-34B-chat, Code Llama-34B, Mistral-7B-chat, WizardLM-13B. Proprietary: GPT-4, GPT-3.5-turbo, Claude-instant-v1, Claude-v1, Claude-v2, plus the three RAG-only systems. Tasks: HellaSwag, Winogrande, ARC-Challenge, MMLU, MT-Bench, GSM8K, MBPP, and an 800-query RAG set collected from one Martian client's search traffic, with hand-collected ground truth.

The oracle router picks, per example, the cheapest model among those with the best quality.

Two router families are evaluated:

- **Predictive.** No LLM is called before the choice. The score is predicted performance minus λ times a cost. The paper approximates cost by a cost-per-token figure. λ is the willingness to pay. Performance is predicted by k nearest neighbors or by an MLP, trained per task on 70% of the rows and tested on 30%. MT-Bench is left out of this split because it is too small. The kNN estimate uses the best model among the neighbors.
- **Non-predictive cascade.** Models run from cheap to expensive. A judge with a threshold stops the cascade. In the paper the judge is given the true score, then corrupted with an error rate ε. A separate overgenerate-and-rerank baseline calls every model. These need a model call before they can decide.

## Key results

- The oracle is near the best quality at low cost. GPT-4 is rarely the oracle's choice. WizardLM-13B and Mistral-7B, about 50% accurate, are chosen often when they happen to be right, because they are cheap. The paper flags over-alignment of some proprietary models (Claude 2 is the example in Appendix C) as one reason a costly model is not always the best answer.
- On the predictive split, kNN and MLP reach the best individual models' quality at similar or lower cost. They beat the Zero router's AIQ on MMLU and Winogrande, match it on HellaSwag and GSM8K, and lose on ARC-Challenge and MBPP. The oracle remains above all of them.
- Cascades with a judge error rate up to 0.1 beat individual models and the Zero router on MMLU, MBPP, and GSM8K, and a zero-error judge approaches the oracle. Above an error rate of 0.2 the cascade can fall off quickly. The paper presents the zero-error curve as an upper bound, not as a deployable judge.
- On the RAG slice (70/30, cascade error rate 0.2), every router beats the Zero router. The paper says the routers pick up time-sensitive cues such as "2024" and send those queries to online models, and send other queries to GPT-4 or GPT-3.5.

Willingness to pay λ is a fixed scalar. Cost enters as a cost-per-token approximation inside the predictive score. The paper does not describe replacing that cost when a vendor changes a rate.
