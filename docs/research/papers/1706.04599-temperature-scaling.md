## Abstract

> Confidence calibration – the problem of predicting probability estimates representative of the true correctness likelihood – is important for classification models in many applications. We discover that modern neural networks, unlike those from a decade ago, are poorly calibrated. Through extensive experiments, we observe that depth, width, weight decay, and Batch Normalization are important factors influencing calibration. We evaluate the performance of various post-processing calibration methods on state-of-the-art architectures with image and document classification datasets. Our analysis and experiments not only offer insights into neural network learning, but also provide a simple and straightforward recipe for practical settings: on most datasets, temperature scaling – a single-parameter variant of Platt Scaling – is surprisingly effective at calibrating predictions.

## Method

Calibration here means that a predicted confidence matches the frequency of being correct. The paper measures that with reliability diagrams, expected calibration error (ECE), maximum calibration error, and negative log likelihood. Every method is a post-process on a held-out validation set. The network weights stay fixed. Train, validation, and test are assumed to be drawn from the same distribution.

Binary methods they compare: histogram binning, isotonic regression, Bayesian binning into quantiles, and Platt scaling (two scalars a and b on the logit, then a sigmoid). Multiclass extensions: one-versus-all binning, matrix scaling (a full linear map of the logit vector), vector scaling (a diagonal map), and temperature scaling.

Temperature scaling divides the logit vector by a single scalar T > 0 and then applies softmax. T > 1 softens the distribution. T = 1 leaves it unchanged. As T grows, the distribution moves toward uniform. As T approaches 0, it collapses to a point mass. T is fit by minimizing negative log likelihood on the validation set. Because T does not change which logit is largest, the predicted class does not change. The paper notes the same form is used in distillation and defines T so the softmax sees logits over T.

## Key results

Vision models are ResNets, stochastic-depth ResNets, Wide ResNets, and DenseNets on Birds, Cars, ImageNet, CIFAR-10/100, and SVHN. Document models are deep averaging networks on 20 Newsgroups and Reuters, and TreeLSTMs on the Stanford Sentiment Treebank.

- Most datasets and models are miscalibrated. ECE, with 15 bins, is typically 4% to 10%. The exceptions are SVHN and Reuters, both under 1% ECE, and both already low-error (1.98% and 2.97%).
- Temperature scaling beats the other methods on the vision tasks and matches them on the NLP tasks. It also beats vector and matrix scaling even though those families contain it. The learned vector scaling is nearly a constant vector, which the paper reads as evidence that the miscalibration is low-dimensional.
- Reuters is the dataset temperature scaling does not help. It is already at ECE ≤ 1%.
- Matrix scaling does poorly on Birds, Cars, and CIFAR-100, and does not converge on 1,000-class ImageNet. The parameter count grows with the square of the class count and overfits a small validation set.
- Binning improves ECE on most datasets but does not beat temperature scaling, and it can change the predicted class, which hurts accuracy. Histogram binning, the simplest of the three, usually beats isotonic regression and Bayesian binning.
- On a 110-layer ResNet on CIFAR-100, the uncalibrated model is overconfident. Temperature scaling recovers the diagonal of the reliability diagram more closely than histogram binning or isotonic regression.
- Temperature scaling is a one-dimensional convex problem. A conjugate-gradient solve finishes in about 10 iterations. Vector scaling on CIFAR-100 (100 classes) takes at least two orders of magnitude longer.

The paper is about classification confidence on vision and document tasks. It does not discuss LLM routing or prices.
